## Title

**SupplyGen: Signal-Triaged Multi-Agent Orchestration with Human-in-the-Loop Guardrails for
Disruption-Aware Inventory Replenishment in E-Commerce Supply Chains**

## Keywords

agentic workflows; inventory replenishment; supply-chain disruption; signal triage;
human-in-the-loop; LangGraph; explainable decision logging; supplier selection

## Abstract (247 words including the four labels)

**Purpose:** Static reorder-point policies in e-commerce replenishment react to stock levels
alone and ignore external warnings such as supplier shutdowns, commodity shortages, severe
weather and seasonal demand. This work examines whether a triaged multi-agent workflow can act
on such signals while keeping computation and human approval effort small.

**Design/Approach/Methods:** SupplyGen is a prototype built with LangGraph and traced with
LangSmith. A signal-triage classifier routes only at-risk stock-keeping-unit (SKU) days to a
Council of Agents (Signal Analyst, Memory-Recall, Demand Forecaster, Supplier Evaluator,
Replenishment Planner); a Supervisor enforces guardrails and escalates sensitive orders to a
human. It is compared with static, moving-average and reactive dual-sourcing baselines under
four disruption scenarios, 30 replications each, on a synthetic dataset and on real demand
replayed from the UCI Online Retail II dataset.

**Findings:** On synthetic demand the Stockout Burden Score fell by 85.4% ± 7.2% against the
static policy and 76.1% against reactive dual sourcing. On real demand the reductions were
50.1% ± 18.4% and 32.1%, and critical-SKU availability during disruptions rose from 86.4% to
99.5%. The Council was invoked on 2.5% to 4.2% of SKU-days. On real demand a static policy
with 21% more safety inventory recovered most of the aggregate gain (42.4%) but not the
critical-SKU availability (90.0%).

**Originality/Value:** The study separates the value of signal-aware decisions from that of
larger buffers, quantifies each agent role by ablation, and measures a local language model as
a drop-in planner. The supplier side and the disruptions remain simulated.

## Methodology

### 1. Dataset

The dataset is synthetic and generated by `supplygen/datagen.py` with a fixed seed (42). It is
stored in SQLite (`data/supplygen.db`) and exported to CSV (`data/csv/`).

| Table | Rows | Content |
|---|---|---|
| categories | 3 | Electronics & Accessories; Grocery & Cooking Essentials; Apparel & Festive, each tied to a commodity |
| suppliers | 8 | region, category, commodity dependency, lead time (3 to 11 days), reliability (0.87 to 0.95), cost multiplier (0.93 to 1.10) |
| skus | 30 | category, priority tier (10 critical, 12 standard, 8 low), base demand (6 to 40 units/day), unit cost, primary supplier |
| sku_suppliers | 80 | approved suppliers per SKU (all suppliers of its category, one primary) |
| demand_history | 10,950 | 365 days x 30 SKUs |
| memory_episodes | 36 | observed effects of past disruptions (3 per type and category) |

Daily demand of SKU *i* on day *t* follows

(1)  D(i,t) ~ Poisson( λ_i · w_dow(t) · m_c(i),t )

where λ_i is base demand, w is a day-of-week factor (0.92 to 1.12) and m is the festival
multiplier of the SKU's category (1 outside festive windows).

**Real-demand dataset.** A second dataset replaces Equation 1 with observed demand from UCI
Online Retail II (Chen, 2019; CC BY 4.0), the invoices of a UK online gift retailer from
1 December 2009 to 9 December 2011. Cancelled invoices and non-product codes are removed, units
are summed per product and day, and the 30 products sold most regularly in both years are kept
(mean 51.7 units per product-day, coefficient of variation above 1, 23% zero-sales days).
Daily values above each product's 99.5th percentile are capped to remove bulk-order outliers.
The first 365 days form the demand history; each run replays a 120-day window drawn from the
remaining 374 days. The dataset has no supplier or priority data, so the 30 products are mapped
onto the synthetic supplier, category and priority layout, and the same disruptions are
injected. Scenario D uses the retailer's real pre-Christmas season (from 1 October 2011) with
no synthetic multiplier; the seasonal uplift in memory is the ratio observed in October and
November 2010 (1.21 to 1.71 by category).

### 2. Stress scenarios and signals

Each run lasts 120 days and contains one real disruption plus eight false alarms (six weak
reports, two loud but low-credibility rumours).

| Scenario | Real disruption | Ground-truth effect (severity s in [0.65, 0.95]) | Warning |
|---|---|---|---|
| A Commodity shortage | edible-oil shortage, 30 to 45 days | lead time x (1 + 1.5s), delivered quantity x (1 - 0.6s) for both oil suppliers | 3 to 7 days before |
| B Supplier failure | largest electronics supplier stops, 20 + 20s days | orders ship only after the failure ends | 0 to 2 days after onset |
| C Weather disruption | IN-South region, 12 to 18 days | lead time + (4 + 10s) days for two suppliers | 2 to 4 days before |
| D Festival surge | 12 to 18 days | demand x (1 + ks), k = 1.8 apparel, 0.7 electronics, 0.5 grocery | 28 days before |

Every signal is normalised to the fields type, affected supplier / region / commodity /
category, window, severity, credibility and a headline. Agents never see the ground truth.

### 3. Baselines

Static reorder point (ROP) and order-up-to level, from the 12-month history:

(2)  ROP_i = μ_i L_i + z σ_i √L_i ,   S_i = ROP_i + μ_i R

with z = 1.65, review period R = 14 days and L_i the primary supplier's lead time. An order of
S_i − IP_i is placed with the primary supplier when the inventory position IP_i ≤ ROP_i.
The Adaptive ROP baseline recomputes μ (14-day window) and σ (28-day window) daily; it is
demand-adaptive but signal-blind.

The Reactive dual-source baseline is the strongest comparator. It sees no external signals but
learns from its own orders: an order not received two days after its quoted lead time marks
that supplier as degraded for 14 days; while degraded, the stuck quantity is not counted as
incoming stock and replenishment goes to the fastest healthy approved supplier, with the
reorder point recomputed for that supplier's lead time.

### 4. Signal triage

(3)  r_s = severity_s · credibility_s

A SKU-day is routed to the Council when a signal touching its primary supplier or category has
r_s ≥ τ (default 0.5) and the inventory position is below a risk-adjusted need:

(4)  N_i = ROP_i + r · R · μ_i                         (supply-side signal)
     N_i = μ_i (H + n_f · s) + z σ_i √L_i ,  H = L_i + round(r R)   (festival signal)

where n_f is the number of festival days inside the next H days. A SKU reviewed within the last
5 days is not re-flagged unless IP_i ≤ ROP_i. All other SKU-days follow Equation 2.

### 5. Council of Agents (LangGraph state graph)

Nodes exchange structured dictionaries; the graph is
Signal Analyst → Memory-Recall → Demand Forecaster → Supplier Evaluator → Replenishment Planner
→ Supervisor → (Human approval) → Audit logger.

- **Signal Analyst** keeps signals with r_s ≥ τ and maps them to the approved suppliers they hit.
- **Memory-Recall** retrieves past episodes of the same type and category and estimates the
  effect per unit of severity, for example for lead time

  (5)  β̂ = mean_k( (lead_mult_k − 1) / s_k ),   estimated multiplier = 1 + β̂ · s

  Without precedent, generic priors are used (lead x (1 + 0.6s), +6s days, fill 1 − 0.25s,
  demand x (1 + 0.5s)).
- **Demand Forecaster**

  (6)  d̂_t = b · u_t ,  b = ½ μ_i + ½ m̄_14 ,  u_t = 1 + β̂_demand · s inside the festive window, else 1

- **Supplier Evaluator** scores each approved supplier *j* (lower is better); the primary is
  left only when it is itself at risk:

  (7)  score_j = ( L_eff,j / φ_j ) · ( 1 + 2 max(0, π_j) ) / ρ_j

  with effective lead time L_eff, expected fill φ, cost premium π over the primary and
  reliability ρ.
- **Replenishment Planner**

  (8)  Q = ⌈ max(0, Σ_{t..t+H−1} d̂ + z σ_i √L_eff · κ_tier − IP_i) / φ ⌉ ,  H = min(L_eff + R, H_max)

  κ = 1.3 / 1.0 / 0.8 for critical / standard / low. If the chosen supplier faces an upcoming
  disruption, H is extended to the end of that window (pre-build). Q below 25% of a normal
  cycle order becomes "hold".

**LLM planner variant.** The Replenishment Planner can be replaced by a local language model
(qwen3:8b through Ollama, temperature 0, JSON output). It receives the same facts as the rule
(stock position, forecast, surge windows, and for each supplier the delivery time, expected
fill, price premium and disruption status) and returns supplier, quantity and a one-sentence
reason. Invalid output falls back to Equation 8, and the Supervisor checks the result either way.

### 6. Supervisor guardrails and human-in-the-loop

(9)  Q ≤ H_max · μ_i · u_max  (H_max = 60 days; otherwise clipped)
     supplier ∈ approved list and π ≤ 25%  (otherwise reverted to primary)
     escalate to a human if order value > 30,000, or a critical SKU changes supplier, or π ≥ 10%

Escalation uses a LangGraph interrupt. In the benchmark a simulated approver accepts every
request (escalations are counted); `run_demo.py --interactive` asks a real person, and a
rejection results in no order. The audit logger writes action, quantity, supplier, supervisor
status, approval result and a rationale built from all agent outputs to `decisions_log`.

### 7. Stock monitoring

Stock is monitored as a daily snapshot per SKU (`inventory_state`: on hand, on order, demand,
unmet demand) in the SQLite warehouse. The controller reads inventory position and days of
cover (IP / μ) each simulated day. In a deployment the same queries would run against an ERP or
warehouse system through the same table contract.

### 8. Evaluation metrics

(10)  SBS = Σ_t Σ_i w_i · U(i,t)        w = 3 / 2 / 1 for critical / standard / low, U = unmet units
(11)  Reduction = 100 · (SBS_static − SBS_controller) / SBS_static      (paired by scenario and seed)
(12)  Critical availability = share of critical-SKU days with U = 0 from disruption start to end + 10 days,
      over critical SKUs whose primary supplier is hit (all critical SKUs for the festival)

SBS weights lost units by priority, so one missing unit of a critical SKU counts three times a
low-priority one. Also reported: fill rate, critical protection rate (share of affected
critical SKUs with no stockout at all), council invocation rate, triage precision (share of
invocations that involve the real disruption), decision latency (triage flag to decision
record), human escalations, total cost (purchase + 0.1%/day holding + 40 per order), cost per
unit sold and average inventory.

## Discussion

**Key findings.** Acting on signals cut priority-weighted stockouts on both datasets, but the
size of the effect depends strongly on the demand. On smooth synthetic demand the reduction
against the static policy is 85.4%; on real, lumpy demand it is 50.1%. The real-demand figure
is the one to quote as the expected effect.

**Signals versus buffers.** On synthetic demand, extra safety stock cannot replace signals: a
static policy with the same extra inventory (z = 4, +19.2%) removes 25.9% of the burden against
85.4%. On real demand this no longer holds for the aggregate score. A static policy with z = 2.5
(+21.0% inventory) removes 42.4%, close to SupplyGen's 50.1% at +16.3%, and z = 3 (+33.9%)
removes more (57.3%). The reason is that most of the real-demand burden is ordinary volatility
on SKUs the disruption does not touch, which only buffers address. What buffers do not buy is
protection of the affected critical SKUs: critical availability stays at 90.0% to 93.6% for
z = 2.5 to 4, against 99.5% for SupplyGen. The contribution is therefore targeted protection
during disruptions, not a general reduction of stockouts.

**Against a reactive baseline.** Reactive dual sourcing, which needs no signals, already removes
29.5% (synthetic) and 22.9% (real) of the burden. SupplyGen is better in 120 of 120 synthetic
and 113 of 120 real pairs (76.1% and 32.1% lower). Its advantage is largest where warning comes
before the delay can be observed (weather, festival) and smallest for supplier failure on real
demand (12,848 against 10,080), where a reactive rule recovers quickly on its own.

**Which component matters.** The Supplier Evaluator is the most valuable role on both datasets
(removing it: 85.4% → 62.2% synthetic, 50.1% → 31.7% real), followed by the Demand Forecaster
and Memory-Recall.

**Triage.** Running the Council on every SKU-day gives the best score (100% synthetic, 90.1%
real) but needs 24 to 40 times more council invocations and 52% to 79% more inventory. Triage
keeps most of the disruption benefit at 2.5% to 4.2% of the invocations. Performance is flat for
τ between 0.1 and 0.6 and collapses above 0.7 on both datasets.

**Adaptive ROP is worse than static on synthetic demand** (−16.2%) because its 28-day σ excludes
last year's festive peak; on real demand it is better than static (10.4%) but holds 23.7% more
inventory.

**Human workload.** The commodity-shortage scenario generates 18 to 28 approval requests per run
because the only unaffected supplier is 10% more expensive, which is the escalation limit. This
is a realistic approval-fatigue risk and argues for batching approvals per supplier.

**Language model as planner.** With the same facts as the rule, the local model
always returned valid output, never chose a disrupted supplier and stayed inside the quantity
guardrail, so the Supervisor had nothing to correct. It was reliable for supply-side disruptions
(same supplier in 39 of 45 cases; the six differences were unnecessary switches on SKUs the
shortage did not affect). It was unreliable for the demand surge: in all nine festival cases it
switched to a faster supplier without a supply reason and ordered a median of 44% of the
rule-based quantity, which would under-stock the surge. At 22.5 s per decision on CPU it is
usable only because triage limits the council to a few percent of SKU-days. This supports a
hybrid design: language models for interpreting signals and explaining decisions, rules or
optimisation for quantities, and guardrails around both.

## Limitations

1. **The supplier side is simulated on both datasets.** Suppliers, lead times, priorities and
   the disruptions themselves are synthetic; only demand is real in the second dataset. No
   real disruption with its actual timeline has been replayed.
2. **The simulator and the agents' assumptions come from the same author**, signals arrive
   already structured, and supply-side memory is seeded with noisy observations of the same
   effect functions. This favours the proposed system.
3. **The LLM was evaluated at decision level only** (54 reorder moments, one model, CPU). The
   120-run benchmarks use the rule-based planner; no closed-loop LLM run was made.
4. **Published disruption windows are exact.** Real signals rarely state an end date.
5. **No capacity limits, price changes during shortages, minimum order quantities or budgets.**
6. **Human approval is simulated as instant and always positive** in the benchmarks.
7. **Baselines are heuristics.** There is no comparison with an optimisation-based or learned
   inventory policy, or with a published agentic method.
8. **No ERP integration.** Purchase orders are rows in SQLite.
9. **Live ingestion was demonstrated once**: the festival calendar and Open-Meteo responded,
   GDELT returned HTTP 429 (rate limit) and FRED was not configured.

## Conclusion (draft)

SupplyGen shows that a triaged, guard-railed multi-agent workflow can turn external warning
signals into protective replenishment decisions. Against a static reorder-point controller it
reduced priority-weighted stockout burden by 85.4% on synthetic demand and by 50.1% on real
retail demand, and against a reactive dual-sourcing baseline by 76.1% and 32.1%. Critical SKUs
stayed available on 99.5% or more of disruption days on both datasets, with the full council
needed on 2.5% to 4.2% of SKU-days. On real demand, larger safety stocks match most of the
aggregate gain but not the protection of critical SKUs, so the benefit is specific to
disruption periods. The supplier side and the disruptions are simulated, so the result
establishes the orchestration pattern and its trade-offs, not production performance.

## Future scope (draft)

Run the LLM planner closed-loop over full simulations and extend it to the other agent roles;
replay a documented disruption with its real timeline and prices; add supplier capacity, budgets
and stochastic disruption end dates; batch approvals; implement the ERP adapter; compare with
an optimisation or reinforcement-learning inventory policy.

### Table 9. Conceptual comparison of replenishment control approaches

| Approach | Control foundation | How it adapts | Interpretability | Main operational trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Static reorder point (baseline) | Fixed reorder point and order-up-to level from history | Does not adapt | Transparent formula | Cheap and predictable; blind to supplier and demand shocks |
| Moving-average reorder point (baseline) | Reorder point re-estimated from recent sales | Follows demand after it changes | Transparent formula | Tracks demand drift; blind to supply shocks, loses seasonal memory |
| Reactive dual sourcing (baseline) | Reorder point plus a switch rule on overdue deliveries | Reacts once a delivery is late | Transparent rule | No signals needed; acts only after the damage has started |
| Optimisation or learned policy (not evaluated) | Stochastic optimisation or reinforcement learning over a demand and lead-time model | Re-solved or retrained on new data | Low for learned policies | Strong when the model holds; needs retraining for unseen disruption types |
| SupplyGen, rule-based council (proposed) | Triage plus five specialised agents under Supervisor guardrails | Acts on external signals before and during the disruption | Structured rationale for every decision | Depends on signal quality; more inventory and human approvals |
| SupplyGen, LLM planner (proposed variant) | Same graph, language model chooses supplier and quantity | Same, with free-text reasoning | Natural-language reason | Seconds per decision; unreliable quantities for demand surges |

The "not evaluated" row is a paradigm, not a measured system, and needs citations from the literature review.
